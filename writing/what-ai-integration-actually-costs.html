What an AI integration project actually costs in 2026.

Honest pricing for adding AI to a product that already has users. The bands a senior shop will quote, the variables that move the price, and the parts that founders consistently underestimate.

Sagar Sutaria Published 4 May 2026 10 min read

TLDR

A real AI integration into a shipped product runs €25K to €80K in a senior shop, six to twelve weeks, depending on what kind of AI feature and how clean your data is. Below €15K and the work is either a Streamlit demo or a wrapper around an LLM API. Above €100K and you are paying for an org chart, not the integration. Most of the price is in evaluation, not in the model call.

Founders ask "how much will it cost to add AI to my product" the same way they used to ask "how much will it cost to add a mobile app." The honest answer in both cases is "what kind, and what state is your data in." The honest answer in 2026 is bounded enough to put numbers on, which is what this post does.

What follows is the pricing reality at a senior shop, not a junior contracting rate. The numbers reflect production-grade work: a feature that ships, has evaluations behind it, has cost controls, and does not embarrass you when it is wrong on a customer call. The bands below are what a senior boutique shop typically quotes across the engagement shapes I have scoped or shipped in the last 18 months. Your own quote will land inside its band based on the variables in the section after.

The four shapes of AI integration

Most projects sort into four shapes. The price band is set by the shape, the data state, and how much of the existing product needs to change to host the feature.

1. RAG over your own data

Retrieval-augmented generation over a company corpus: docs, product data, ticketing history, internal wiki. The model answers user questions by retrieving relevant context first, then generating against it.

Price band: €25K to €55K. Six to ten weeks.

The price moves on data quality. If your corpus is markdown in a tidy git repo, the lower band is reachable. If your data is across Notion, Confluence, Slack, a Postgres database, and three SaaS exports nobody has touched in two years, the price moves up. The expensive part is the retrieval and re-ranking pipeline, not the LLM call.

2. Agentic workflows

An agent that takes user intent and executes a multi-step workflow against your tools or APIs. Common patterns: support ticket triage and resolution drafting, ops dashboard automation, sales follow-up sequences with judgment, internal knowledge agents.

Price band: €35K to €75K. Eight to twelve weeks.

The price moves on the action surface. An agent that can take ten actions is fundamentally different from an agent that can take three. Each action needs an evaluation, error handling, an audit trail, and a rollback story. Most price overruns on agent projects come from underestimating how much engineering goes into making each action safe.

3. Evaluation harness for an existing AI feature

You already shipped an AI feature. It works most of the time. You cannot tell why it works when it works or fails when it fails. Cannot regression-test. Cannot compare model versions. Cannot defend it to enterprise buyers who ask.

Price band: €20K to €40K. Four to six weeks.

Often the highest-leverage AI work a company can pay for. An evaluation harness turns a black box into something measurable. The price band is tighter because the deliverable is concrete: a test set, a scoring rubric, a CI hook, and a dashboard.

4. Model migration off vendor lock

Existing AI feature pinned to one provider. Pricing or SLA pressure forces a migration. The work is benchmark, parity test, prompt portability, fallback strategy, and cost rebalance.

Price band: €25K to €50K. Five to eight weeks.

Drives ROI through unit economics. A successful migration commonly cuts inference cost by 35 to 60 percent when the original implementation was tuned for one provider and never benchmarked since.

The four parts founders underestimate

Across a hundred quotes, the same four cost categories get under-priced when teams plan their own builds.

Evaluation. Most teams budget the model call and the integration. They do not budget the test set, the regression harness, or the human review cycle that catches drift in production. 20 to 30 percent of the project belongs here. Skipping it is how teams ship a feature that works in demo and degrades in three weeks.

Cost controls. Token budgets, caching, request batching, model routing. Without these the LLM bill in production routinely runs three to five times the demo bill, and your gross margin gets quietly eaten. Budget 10 percent of the project for cost engineering. It pays back inside the first quarter.

Failure paths. What happens when the LLM is down. When it returns malformed output. When it returns content that breaks your downstream code. When it generates output the user should not see. Budget 10 to 15 percent for failure handling, including the UI work for graceful degradation.

Vendor lock decisions. Choosing a provider is a six-month commitment, often longer. The audit before the choice matters more than the integration. Most teams skip this entirely and pay for it on month nine when pricing changes or capacity dries up.

What the cheap quote is hiding

If a vendor quotes you €8K for an AI integration, you are getting one of three things: a wrapper around the OpenAI API with a prompt in it, an open-source RAG template configured for your data with no evaluation, or a Streamlit demo that will never make production. Any of those can be the right move if you know that is what you are buying. None of them is an AI integration into a shipped product.

If a vendor quotes you €200K and twelve months for the same project, you are buying an org chart. There is no AI feature category that needs twelve months of senior engineering at a four-engineer team rate to ship. Walk.

How to keep the price honest

Two things keep an AI integration project priced correctly.

One, fixed scope locked at the SOW with written change orders. Time and materials on AI projects encourages exploration that does not ship. Pin the scope. Write down what done means. Pay against milestones, not hours.

Two, a 20 percent milestone holdback at the final acceptance gate. Released only after the agreed evaluation criteria are met in production, not in staging. This single mechanism eliminates the "demo works, production does not" failure mode more reliably than any other contracting choice.
