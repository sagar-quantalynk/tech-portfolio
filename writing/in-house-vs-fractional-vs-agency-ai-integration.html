In-house vs fractional vs agency: who should run your AI integration?

Three paths for shipping the AI feature on your roadmap. Hire an in-house ML engineer. Retain a fractional CTO whose first deliverable is a paid audit. Engage an agency. They are not interchangeable, and the decision is not about cost. It is about who is responsible when the integration stalls in week six.

Sagar Sutaria Published 6 May 2026 10 min read

Disclosure first, since the rest of the essay reads in light of it. I have hired in-house ML engineers, run AI integrations as a fractional CTO, and bought from agencies as a client. I currently sell the fractional path through QuantaLynk. The point of this comparison is to give you the working test, not to push you to fractional. The recommendation at the end concedes where in-house wins and where agency wins. If your situation matches one of those concessions, take that path.

TLDR

For an angel-to-Series-A company with one AI integration on the roadmap and a founder who can hold a 90-minute architectural conversation, the fractional path with a paid audit usually wins on cost, time-to-first-value, and accountability. In-house wins post-Series-B when AI is product, not feature. Agency wins when integrations run in parallel across teams, or when the founder has zero capacity to manage a vendor relationship and needs a project manager contractually on the hook.

The three shapes.

Buyers conflate these three because the SERP groups them. They are not the same shape of help. The first decision is shape, not vendor.

Headcount model

In-house ML engineer

A senior engineer with ML or applied-AI experience on payroll. Reports to the founder or to the head of engineering. Owns architecture, build, and ongoing operation.

  • Salary band 140 to 220K total comp, EU senior
  • 12 to 16 week ramp before first ship
  • Owns architecture and operates the integration
  • You manage them; they manage themselves between sprints
  • One person, one calendar, one risk vector
Retainer model

Fractional CTO + paid audit

A senior operator who runs point on the integration on retainer. The engagement opens with a 2-week paid audit. Build follows, fixed scope, milestone holdback.

  • Audit from 8K, 2 weeks. Build from 25K, 6 to 10 weeks
  • First deliverable in week 2 (the audit), shipped feature 6 to 12 weeks later
  • Owns architecture and decision velocity
  • Subcontracts to senior engineers for build hands
  • One accountable counterparty, written SOW
Vendor model

AI agency

A 30 to 80 person AI shop, or a named AI practice inside a larger consultancy. Engagement runs against a SOW with a delivery PM, lead engineer, and supporting bench.

  • Engagement band 80 to 250K, 3 to 6 months
  • 4 to 8 weeks from contract to first ship
  • PM contractually on the hook for delivery
  • Bench depth handles parallel workstreams
  • Change orders billed; scope locked at SOW

When each one wins.

The matrix is structured. The honest version of the recommendation is unstructured. Each path wins in a specific shape of company.

Default for the cohort

Fractional + audit wins when

  • You are angel through Series A, with one AI integration on the roadmap and no other live AI work.
  • The founder or a senior engineer can hold a 90-minute architectural conversation without a translator.
  • You want a written assessment before you commit to a build (the audit is a qualified-out gate, not a sales pitch).
  • You want fixed scope, milestone holdback, and one accountable counterparty rather than time-and-materials billing.
  • You expect 1 to 3 AI integrations over the next 18 months, not 5+.
Past a clear threshold

In-house ML engineer wins when

  • You are post-Series-B and AI is product, not feature. The integration is the first of many that will be operated by the same team.
  • Your roadmap names a model-research arm, fine-tuning workstream, or evaluation infrastructure that runs continuously.
  • You have an existing engineering org of 8+ that can absorb the new hire without disrupting existing delivery.
  • You are comfortable with 14 to 20 weeks before first ship because the long-term cost curve flattens.
  • Your data, models, or domain are too proprietary to run through a vendor relationship.
Two narrow shapes

An AI agency wins when

  • You have multiple AI integrations running in parallel across product teams and you need a delivery PM contractually on the hook for each.
  • You are a non-technical founder with no engineering team, no senior contractor on staff, and no capacity to absorb the architectural conversation. Fractional fails here. The agency model assumes nothing of the buyer.
  • You have institutional procurement that requires a multi-headcount vendor on the master services agreement.
  • The first AI integration was attempted internally and stalled. The second attempt benefits from PM discipline more than from senior architectural judgment.

The working test.

The matrix and the wins-when blocks are scaffolding. The actual decision usually collapses to one question.

Q1: How many AI integrations will your company ship in the next 18 months?
One. Fractional + audit. The economics of in-house do not pencil for one ship. The contracting overhead and bench cost of an agency does not either. A 2-week audit followed by a 6 to 10 week build, with milestone holdback, is the shape that fits.
Two to four, sequential. Fractional still wins. A single retainer covers the architectural through-line across integrations. Cost stays linear, not stepwise.
Two to four, parallel across product teams. Agency wins. The bench handles parallelism, the PM holds the through-line. Fractional caps at sequential.
Five or more, or AI is the product. In-house. Hire the ML lead, build the team under them, scope the first integration as their onboarding project. Fractional and agency are both wrong shapes at this volume.

What the matrix doesn't say.

Three things the comparison glosses that change the call in real engagements.

The audit is a qualifier, not a contract. The 2-week audit at the head of the fractional path is not a sales gate. It is a paid deliverable: a written assessment of feasibility, cost band, named risks, and a go/no-go recommendation. Half of the audits in my experience produce a "do not build this yet" or "build this differently" recommendation. The audit costs less than the contracted hours of a senior in-house hire's first month. If you can afford 8 to 12K to find out whether the integration is worth doing at all, the audit is structurally cheaper than committing to any of the three paths blind. More on the audit shape and what's in scope.

Cost is more variable than the matrix suggests. RAG integrations land around 25 to 35K on the fractional path, agentic workflows 35 to 50K, regulated verticals add 10 to 20K of compliance overhead. Agency engagement bands shift more dramatically by vertical and by named PM. The cost essay covers the variance in detail; read the breakdown there rather than re-litigating it on this page. The matrix's cost row is a band, not a quote.

Regulated industries change the math twice. First, all three paths get more expensive (compliance review, audit trails, evaluation overhead). Second, the agency premium often pays for itself because regulated agencies have named compliance practices and the contracts are structured to absorb regulator-facing risk. The regulated-industries essay covers the multipliers and where the audit-first fractional path still works inside HIPAA, GDPR-Article-22, and SR 11-7 environments. If you are in finance, healthcare, or legal, do not pick a path based on this essay alone.

Pricing, side by side.

Numbers a buyer can actually plug into a finance model. Same scope across the three paths: a single RAG-backed AI feature for a SaaS product, no fine-tuning, EU compliance baseline, ship to production with an evaluation harness.

In-house. Hire a senior ML engineer at €170K base + 30K benefits + 10K equipment and tooling = €210K year-one. They start week 8 (hiring lag), ramp to first commit at week 12, ship the integration around week 18. The integration costs the company €210K of payroll plus the opportunity cost of the founder's hiring time. Cost in year two is €210K to operate one integration.

Fractional + audit. €10K audit week 1 to 2. €30 to 45K build weeks 3 to 12 (depending on RAG complexity). €5 to 10K stabilization in weeks 13 to 14. Total €45 to 65K across roughly 14 calendar weeks. Operations after handoff is the founder's existing team, not a recurring cost. Public pricing here.

Agency. €120 to 180K for a 14-week engagement with a delivery PM, lead engineer, and bench depth. Includes scoping (weeks 1 to 3), build (weeks 4 to 11), and stabilization (weeks 12 to 14). Operations after handoff is a separate support contract, typically €4 to 8K/month if you want SLAs.

The price comparison flatters fractional at 1x scope and flatters in-house and agency at higher scopes. If you only ever ship one integration, fractional is roughly a third of in-house and roughly half of an agency. If you ship five integrations across 18 months, in-house is the cheapest of the three. The matrix axis that separates the three is integration count, not unit price.

The bait in this decision.

Buyers fall into a fourth shape that looks like the right answer and is not. The AI strategy advisory retainer. A consultant or boutique firm at €4 to 6K per month, no build attached, no audit, no SOW. You get monthly slide decks, a vendor short-list, and a roadmap. The integration never ships because nobody on the retainer has hands on the codebase.

Three signs you are about to buy advisory-only when you needed build:

  • The contract has no fixed deliverable past month one.
  • The advisor has not asked to see the actual product or codebase.
  • "AI strategy" appears in the SOW without "AI integration" appearing.

If your roadmap names an AI feature, the engagement should ship that feature. Advisory without build is the worst of both worlds: cost without code. None of the three paths in this essay are advisory-only. All three put a shipped feature on the table.

What this doesn't replace.

This essay decides path, not provider. Once you know whether you need fractional, agency, or in-house, the second decision (which fractional, which agency, which engineer) is a different essay. The AI readiness scorecard is the working tool for the next step: it scores your situation across feasibility, data readiness, team capacity, and integration risk before you spend on any of the three paths. If the scorecard shows red on data readiness or team capacity, the path question is premature.
