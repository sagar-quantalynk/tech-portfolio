How long does an AI integration actually take.

A senior operator's timeline for adding AI to a product that already has users. The six phases, the variables that move the schedule, the buyer-side drag founders forget, and the milestone split that keeps it honest.

TLDR

Most fixed-scope AI integrations into a shipped product run 6 to 12 weeks of build, plus an optional 2-week paid audit that decides whether to build at all. Regulated industries add 2 to 4 weeks. Below 6 weeks you are buying a wrapper. Above 16 weeks you are paying for an org chart. Most timeline blowouts I see are buyer-side drag, not engineering.

Founders ask "how long will an AI integration take" the same way they used to ask "how long will it take to add a mobile app." The honest answer in both cases is "what kind, and how clean is your data." The honest answer in 2026 is bounded enough to put weeks on, which is what this post does. A separate post handles the price; this one handles the schedule.

What follows is the timeline reality at a senior shop, not a junior contracting cadence. It assumes a feature that ships, has evaluations behind it, has cost controls, and does not embarrass you when it is wrong on a customer call. Demos are faster. Production is not.

02 What happens in each phase

Phases as artifacts, not durations.

Each phase ends when a specific thing exists, not when a clock runs out.

Phase 02 weeks · optional

Audit

Output: written brief + go or no-go

Two weeks, paid, scoped as its own engagement. The deliverable is a written brief covering the integration shape, the data state, the model choice, the cost ceiling, and the realistic timeline for the build that would follow. The most valuable audit outcome is "do not build this yet." That outcome is structurally impossible inside Phase 1 of a build engagement, which is why the audit is its own SOW. More on the audit.

Phase 1Weeks 1 to 2

Scope and prototype

Output: end-to-end slice behind a feature flag

Pin the scope. Write what done means. Stand up the smallest working slice that touches the real model, the real retrieval path, and the real UI surface, behind a feature flag, in your environment. Not a Streamlit demo. The point is to make the shape of the integration concrete by the end of week two so nobody is arguing about words for the next eight weeks.

Phase 2Weeks 2 to 5

Integration

Output: working integration in your product

Wire into production data, identity, billing, observability. Replace the prototype's shortcuts with the real plumbing. The week-five milestone is a working integration behind a feature flag in your environment that an internal user can route a real request through and get a real answer. That is the moment the 30 percent payment releases.

Phase 3Weeks 4 to 7

Evaluation

Output: test set, scoring rubric, regression CI

Overlaps with integration on purpose. The eval set is built against the real integration, not against a prototype that no longer reflects the product. Twenty-five to thirty percent of the project's labour goes here, and it is the work that decides whether the feature still works in week ten. Skipped evals = ships, degrades quietly, embarrasses someone at month three.

Phase 4Weeks 6 to 9

Production hardening

Output: failure paths, cost ceilings, rollback story

What happens when the model is down, returns malformed output, leaks something it should not, or quietly costs five times what it did last week. Token budgets, caching, model routing, circuit breakers. The work that turns a feature into a product. Most teams under-budget this phase by a factor of two and discover it the week before launch.

Phase 5Weeks 8 to 10

Acceptance

Output: eval green + cost met + 7-day uptime

Three concrete checks, all green at the same time, in production. The eval harness passes the metric set in the SOW. The per-call cost is at or below the model the audit produced. The integration runs cleanly over a rolling seven-day uptime window. The 20 percent acceptance-gate payment releases at this point and not before.

The holdback is the mechanism that keeps schedules honest. A studio that takes 100 percent on signing has no incentive to fight for the acceptance criteria; a studio that has 20 percent at risk fights to land the eval set, hold the cost ceiling, and survive the uptime window. The mechanism is older than AI work; the discipline transfers cleanly.

05 Where founders are wrong

The two-week trap.

The most common timeline conversation in 2026 is a four-to-one mismatch between what the founder thinks the work is and what the work is.

What the founder hears on Twitter
2 weeks

"Cursor + Claude + a weekend, ship it." That number is real for a prototype on a clean dataset that nobody depends on. It is not real for a feature that ships into a product with paying customers, observability, support load, and a CFO who looks at the model bill.

What the work is in production
8 weeks typical

Six to twelve weeks for a real integration. The four-to-one gap is not an engineering inefficiency. It is the work that exists in production but does not exist in a weekend prototype: the eval set, the failure paths, the cost ceilings, the regression harness, the deletion machinery, the launch behind a flag with a rollback story.

The mismatch shows up in two places. First, in scoping, where founders push back on the quote because they read the Twitter thread. Second, in week three, when the demo works and the founder asks why this is not done. The answer in both cases is the same: the demo is the cheap part. Production is what you are paying for.

I tell founders to spend the prototype week before signing the build SOW, not during it. Build a Streamlit demo over a weekend, prove to yourself that the model can do the thing, then commission the integration. The weekend prototype is the easiest way to be honest about whether the feature is worth eight weeks of senior engineering. Most are. A meaningful minority are not, and finding out before the SOW is the whole point of the prototype.

06 Your half of the schedule

The drag nobody quotes.

Most timeline blowouts are not engineering. They are the buyer's internal cycles, and they belong on your project plan, not the studio's.

None of these are the studio's fault. All of them land on the schedule. The fix is the same as for engineering scope: front-load the conversation. The kickoff call is the right place to ask which of these are already in motion, which need to start tomorrow, and which the studio's calendar should plan around. The buyer who answers honestly at kickoff usually ships on time. The buyer who answers vaguely usually does not.

07 If you ask for half

"Can you do this in 4 weeks?"

The honest answer is: yes, with three things removed. Decide which.

Compression menu

To halve a 10-week build to 5 weeks, the studio cuts in this order: the eval harness (the feature ships without regression coverage and degrades inside three months), the second source of data (the integration covers 60 percent of the corpus instead of 100 percent), and the cost-control machinery (per-call costs run unchecked until someone notices the bill).

Any of these can be a deliberate trade. A founder shipping a demo for a fundraise has a different calculus than one shipping for a customer SLA. The risk is not making the trade; the risk is asking for the timeline without naming what gets cut, then being surprised when the cut surfaces in week eight.

The opposite case is also worth stating. A buyer who wants a 16-week timeline because "we want to be careful" is usually scoping an org chart, not an integration. The 6-to-12-week band exists because that is what the work takes. Stretching it does not improve the outcome; it usually means more meetings, more stakeholders, and more chances for scope to grow into the available time.
